注 0.1. Let X be a regular projective surface. (In fact, it is perhaps sufficient to only assume that X is a Noetherian factorial scheme).

定义 0.2 (Notations). Let C and D be effective divisors on X. We define the intersection number of C and D, denoted as (C⋅D), by the following equality:degC​(O(D)∣C​)=−(O(−D)⋅C)=(O(D)⋅C)=(O(D)⋅O(C)⋅X)=degD​(O(C)∣D​).

Furthermore, if we assume that C∩Ass(OD​)=∅, then tensoring the short exact sequence0⟶O(−C)⟶OX​⟶OC​⟶0with OD​ preserves exactness. This implies:degD​(O(C)∣D​)=−degD​(O(−C)∣D​)=χ(OC∩D​)=h0(C∩D,OC∩D​).

注 0.3 (20.2.1). When X is a Cohen-Macaulay surface (for example, a regular surface), there are automatically no embedded associated points. In this case, the condition C∩Ass(OD​)=∅ can be safely replaced by the simpler geometric condition that C and D have no common irreducible components. (Because Ass∖Embedded corresponds to those minimal associated primes.) Especially, if C and D are both irreducible and C=D set-theoretically, then (C⋅D)=h0(C∩D)≥0.

注 0.4 (20.2.3). Recall the definition of the normal bundle NC/X​:=OX​(C)∣C​. Then its degree is given by the self-intersection number: degNC/X​=(C⋅C).

注 0.5 (20.2.4). As seen by the definition, the intersection number (C⋅D) can be expressed in several different ways, each with its own characteristics:

1.

(O(C)⋅O(D)⋅X): This expression is very useful for formal manipulations, but it is geometrically somewhat abstract.

2.

degD​(O(C)∣D​): This expression is not manifestly symmetric.

3.

h0(C∩D,OC∩D​): This expression is geometrically intuitive.

例 0.6. Let X=Ak2​. Consider the curves C=V(y−x2) and D=V(y). The intersection scheme is given by:C∩D≅Spec k[x,y]/(y−x2,y)≅Spec k[x]/(x2).Thus, we have h0=2. This correctly reflects the geometric intuition of intersection multiplicity, as the line D is tangent to the parabola C.

By definition, this intersection pairing induces a bilinear form on the Picard group:Pic(X)×Pic(X)(O(C),O(D))​⟶Z⟼(C⋅D).​

定理 0.7 (Application 1: Recovering Bézout’s Theorem). Let C and D be curves in P2 that share no common components. Then the degree of their intersection is given by:deg(C∩D)=degC⋅degD.

Proof. Let c=degC and d=degD. Since C and D have no common components, the intersection is a proper intersection of dimension zero. The left-hand side is the number of intersection points counted with multiplicity, which equals the Euler characteristic of the intersection scheme:LHS​=h0(C∩D,OC∩D​)=(O(C)⋅O(D)⋅OP2​)=(O(c)⋅O(d)⋅OP2​).​

Using the general formula for the intersection of two line bundles, (L1​⋅L2​)=χ(L1​⊗L2​)−χ(L1​)−χ(L2​)+χ(OX​), and the fact that χ(OP2​(n))=2(n+1)(n+2)​, we can compute:(O(c)⋅O(d))​=χ(O(c+d))−χ(O(c))−χ(O(d))+χ(OP2​)=2(c+d+1)(c+d+2)​−2(c+1)(c+2)​−2(d+1)(d+2)​+1=cd.​This completes the proof.

□

命题 0.8 (Application 2: 20.2.B). Assuming Serre’s duality for a smooth projective surface X, the following holds:

1.

Adjunction Formula: For any curve C⊂X, C⋅(KX​+C)=2pa​(C)−2.

2.

Riemann-Roch for Surfaces: For any divisor D on X, χ(X,OX​(D))=2D⋅(D−KX​)​+χ(X,OX​).

Proof. Proof of (1): Consider the standard short exact sequence 0→OX​(−C)→OX​→OC​→0. We evaluate the intersection number C⋅(−KX​−C) using the definition:C⋅(−KX​−C)=χ(OX​)−χ(OX​(KX​+C))−χ(OX​(−C))+χ(OX​(KX​)).By Serre duality, we know that χ(OX​(KX​+C))=χ(OX​(−C)) and χ(OX​(KX​))=χ(OX​). Substituting these into the equation yields:C⋅(−KX​−C)​=2(χ(OX​)−χ(OX​(−C)))=2χ(OC​)(from the short exact sequence)=2(1−pa​(C)).​Multiplying both sides by −1 gives C⋅(KX​+C)=2pa​(C)−2.

Proof of (2): First, we compute −(D⋅D) by treating it as (D⋅−D):−(D⋅D)=χ(OX​)−χ(OX​(−D))−χ(OX​(D))+χ(OX​)=2χ(OX​)−χ(OX​(D))−χ(OX​(−D)).Next, we compute D⋅KX​ by writing it as (−D)⋅(−KX​):D⋅KX​=(−D)⋅(−KX​)=χ(OX​)−χ(OX​(D))−χ(OX​(KX​))+χ(OX​(KX​+D)).Applying Serre duality again, χ(OX​(KX​))=χ(OX​) and χ(OX​(KX​+D))=χ(OX​(−D)). Thus, the equation simplifies to:D⋅KX​=χ(OX​(−D))−χ(OX​(D)).Adding the expression for −(D⋅D) and D⋅KX​ together, we obtain:D⋅(KX​−D)​=(χ(OX​(−D))−χ(OX​(D)))+(2χ(OX​)−χ(OX​(D))−χ(OX​(−D)))=2χ(OX​)−2χ(OX​(D)).​Rearranging this equation directly recovers the Riemann-Roch formula for surfaces:χ(X,OX​(D))=2D⋅(D−KX​)​+χ(X,OX​).

□

定理 0.9 (20.2.C). We know that Pic(P1×P1)=Z⊕Z. Let l=P1×{0} and m={0}×P1. The line bundles O(l) and O(m) are the generators. Then we have the following intersection numbers:l⋅l=m⋅m=0,l⋅m=1.

Proof. The intersection l∩m is exactly one point (which corresponds to Spec k), so l⋅m=1.

Let l′=P1×{∞}. We have l∩l′=∅, which implies l⋅l′=0. Since l is linearly equivalent to l′, it follows that l⋅l=l⋅l′=0. By a symmetric argument, we also obtain m⋅m=0.

□

Application: Consider the diagonal Δ≅P1↪P1×P1. What are the coefficients a and b such that the class [Δ]=a[l]+b[m]?

By computing the intersection numbers, we have Δ⋅l=1 and Δ⋅m=1. Expanding the intersection yields a=1 and b=1.

定理 0.10 (20.2.D). Let X=Blp​P2 be the blow-up of P2 at a point p. Let l be a line in P2 not passing through p (identified with its strict transform), and let e be the exceptional divisor. Then Pic(X)=Zl⊕Ze, and their intersection numbers are:l⋅l=1,e⋅e=−1,l⋅e=0.

Proof Sketch. This proof relies on a more geometric approach. Consider the blow-up morphism π:Blp​P2→P2. Since the line l⊂P2 does not pass through p, its strict transform naturally avoids the exceptional divisor.

We can find another line l′ in P2 that passes through p. Since any two lines in P2 are linearly equivalent, we have OX​(l)≅OX​(l′). The pullback of l′ is given by π∗l′=e+m, where m is the strict transform of l′.

First, we determine the self-intersection of m. We can find another line l′′ passing through p, whose pullback is π∗l′′=e+m′. Since π∗l′∼π∗l′′, it follows that m∼m′. We can choose l′ and l′′ such that their strict transforms do not intersect, meaning m∩m′=∅. Therefore, m⋅m=0.

Now we compute the remaining intersections:

•

e⋅m=1 (because the strict transform m intersects the exceptional divisor e at exactly one point).

•

l⋅e=0 (because the line l does not pass through p, so its strict transform is disjoint from e).

•

l⋅m=1 (because the intersection l∩l′ is exactly one point in P2 that is distinct from p).

Using these relations, we can deduce the self-intersection of l:l⋅l=l⋅π∗l′=l⋅(e+m)=l⋅e+l⋅m=0+1=1.For the exceptional divisor, since π∗l′∼l, we substitute e=π∗l′−m:e⋅e=(π∗l′−m)⋅e=l⋅e−m⋅e=0−1=−1.

By the excision exact sequence, we have:Ze⟶Pic(X)⟶Pic(X∖e)⟶0.Notice that X∖e≅P2∖{p}. Since the point p has codimension strictly greater than 1 in P2, the Picard group remains unaffected by its removal. Thus, we have:Pic(X∖e)=Pic(P2∖{p})≅Pic(P2)≅Z.Therefore, Pic(X) can be generated by the classes of l and e, and their intersection numbers are as stated.

Finally, from the intersection numbers, we can verify that l and e are linearly independent in Pic(X), confirming that Pic(X)=Zl⊕Ze.

□

Motivated by the above discussion, we have the following definition:

定义 0.11 (20.2.8). Let X be a surface over a field k. Let C⊆X be a curve consisting of smooth points such that C≅Pk1​ and its self-intersection number is (C⋅C)=−1. We call such a curve C a (−1)-curve.

A glimpse: X is a smooth projective surface over C. It is called minimal if it does not contain any rational (i.e. birational to PC1​) (−1)-curves. There is a conclusion that if ∣mKX​∣=0 for some m>0, then X is minimal if and only if KX​ is nef, i.e. (KX​⋅C)≥0 for every curve C⊆X.

注 0.12. By analyzing the generators of Pic(P1×P1) and Pic(Blp​P2) along with their intersection forms, we can deduce that P1×P1≅Blp​P2.

Classical Example (20.2.G): Note that l≅P1≅e. Let us take two copies of Blp​P2, denoted as Y1​ and Y2​. In Y1​, we have the naturally embedded curves l1​ and e1​; in Y2​, we have the curves l2​ and e2​. We construct a new space X by gluing Y1​ and Y2​ along these curves such that l1​ is identified with e2​, and e1​ is identified with l2​.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that X is a projective scheme. Then there exists an ample line bundle L on X. Let L1​ and L2​ be the pullbacks of L via the natural inclusions Y1​↪X and Y2​↪X, respectively.

Since the restriction of an ample line bundle to a closed subscheme remains ample, both L1​ and L2​ must still be ample. Consequently, their intersection with any curve in Y1​ and Y2​ must be strictly positive. In particular, for the curves m1​∼l1​−e1​ and m2​∼l2​−e2​, we have:L1​⋅m1​>0andL2​⋅m2​>0.By linearity, this implies:L1​⋅l1​>L1​⋅e1​andL2​⋅l2​>L2​⋅e2​.However, due to the gluing conditions, the intersection numbers evaluated on the identified curves must be equal:L1​⋅l1​=L2​⋅e2​andL1​⋅e1​=L2​⋅l2​.This immediately leads to a contradiction! (We would obtain L1​⋅l1​>L1​⋅e1​=L2​⋅l2​>L2​⋅e2​=L1​⋅l1​, which is impossible).

Therefore, X is not projective. Since X is constructed by gluing proper schemes along proper closed subschemes, X itself is proper. This provides a classical example showing that a proper scheme is not necessarily projective.
